Shapes under the door

Kerem Zengin · 4 November 2026

Draft. A clickable sketch of Searle’s Chinese room. The story is the famous one. The page does not settle it.

TL;DR

You sit in a room with a book of rules. Slips of paper come in covered in shapes. The book says which shape to send back. From outside, the exchange looks like a conversation. From inside, you never needed to know what any shape meant.

That is the whole pressure of the argument: a procedure can pass a test for understanding and still be only a procedure.

Work the book

Pick a slip. The rule that matches it lights up. Send back the shape the rule names. Wrong shapes are refused. When the right one goes out, the hallway can be read in English. The English was never in the book you used.

Who is supposed to understand?

Searle’s claim is that the person in the room does not understand, and that a program is in the same spot: syntax in, syntax out. A common reply, the systems reply, says the person is the wrong place to look. The understanding, if there is any, belongs to the whole room: person, book, slips, and door.

Another reply, the robot reply, says the room is too closed. Hook the procedure to a body that can see the door and the shapes might stop being only shapes. This page can show the two descriptions. It cannot decide which one is right. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on the Chinese room is the longer map of the replies.
